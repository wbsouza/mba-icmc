\chapter{Methodology} \label{cap:methodology}

This chapter sets out the methodology adopted by the present work, translating the conceptual framework reviewed in Chapter~\ref{cap:theoretical-foundation} into a concrete experimental pipeline. The presentation is organized in eleven sections: the positioning of the present work relative to the closest prior art (Section~\ref{sec:positioning}), an overview of the research design (Section~\ref{sec:research-design}), the data architecture and storage layer (Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture}), the Forex price-data pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:price-pipeline}), the news and event-data pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:news-pipeline}), the sentiment and event feature layer (Section~\ref{sec:feature-layer}), the fusion architecture (Section~\ref{sec:fusion}), the backtesting and evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), the execution engine (Section~\ref{sec:execution-engine}), the reproducibility procedures and threats to validity (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}), and a closing summary (Section~\ref{sec:methodology-final-remarks}).

The methodology is deliberately empirical and engineering-led. Every design choice in this chapter is motivated either by a constraint exposed in Chapter~\ref{cap:theoretical-foundation} (for example, the methodological discipline imposed by financial machine learning \cite{lopezdeprado2018advances}) or by an operational consideration (storage budget, license compatibility, reproducibility). Choices that follow from neither are recorded as open items rather than asserted.

\noindent\textbf{Design versus demonstrated execution.} This chapter defines the target architecture and evaluation protocol. It does not establish that every proposed source, feature, validation procedure, or experiment has been executed. The current runnable study is a fixed-model EUR/USD pilot: family models are fitted on February--June 2015, the combiner is calibrated on July, and later data are reserved for evaluation. Rolling retraining, combinatorial purged cross-validation (CPCV), and an embargo schedule remain unexecuted in this pilot. Section~\ref{subsec:workflow-stages} makes the stage boundaries explicit; Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} records the implementation and evidence available as of September 26, 2026.

\section{Positioning Relative to Prior Art} \label{sec:positioning}

The closest published work to the present methodology is the recent study of \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha}, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}. The design overlap with the present work is substantial. The proposed design shares GDELT, a FinBERT-class scorer, and EUR/USD and USD/JPY with that study, with overlapping target windows (2017 to 2025 there, 2015 to 2024 here). The implemented pilot uses GDELT event records rather than article sentiment and only EUR/USD; it does not reproduce that study. For this reason, the methodology cannot be presented as a self-contained engineering exercise without first declaring how it differs from that benchmark. Six points of divergence are stated up front. Each one is motivated by a constraint that subsequent sections of this chapter elaborate.

\textit{Validation protocol.} \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} adopts a five-fold expanding-window cross-validation scheme on daily features and reports cost-adjusted Sharpe ratios of 5.87 on EUR/USD and 4.65 on USD/JPY. Comparing these figures requires matching the sampling convention, costs, and validation design, and accounting for selection bias and backtest overfitting \cite{lopezdeprado2018advances}. According to that discipline, the target protocol includes combinatorial purged $k$-fold cross-validation in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, with an explicit embargo period between training and validation folds and a single held-out test pass per strategy variant. This is a design objective, not evidence of stronger validation in the current pilot; no direction or magnitude of the resulting Sharpe difference is assumed in advance.

\textit{Temporal resolution.} The scorer in \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} aggregates GDELT articles into daily indices and the trading model emits daily decisions. The present work runs the price layer at minute resolution. Sentiment features are produced per article and bucketed onto the minute grid using each article's publication timestamp (Section~\ref{subsec:sentiment-features}), so the intraday arrival pattern of news is preserved on the sentiment side. Event-aggregate features (CAMEO category counts, GPR regime) remain at daily resolution and are forward-filled onto the minute grid (Section~\ref{subsec:event-features}); the gain over \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} on those features is in the price-execution wrapper around them, not in their own arrival timing. The asymmetry is documented as a feature-engineering choice rather than asserted as a uniform intraday upgrade.

\textit{Feature breadth on the price side.} \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} does not include chart-and-candlestick pattern features or a structured technical-analysis stack on top of the price input. The target design includes the four-family price input space described in Section~\ref{sec:feature-layer}: technical-analysis structural features, indicator features, candlestick-pattern detectors from \cite{nison1991candlestick} via the TA-Lib reference implementation, and classical chart patterns following the kernel-regression detection procedure of \citeonline{lo2000foundations}. The motivation is the position taken in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}: hybrid strategies should evaluate the marginal contribution of textual features against a price-only baseline that is itself competitive, rather than a degenerate one.

\textit{Feature breadth on the text side.} In addition to the FinBERT scorer, the target design also includes a Loughran--McDonald dictionary scorer \cite{loughran2011liability} as a transparent baseline. Furthermore, the GPR continuous regime index of \citeonline{caldara2022geopolitical} complements GDELT's discrete event records, and additional corpora (FNSPID \cite{dong2024fnspid}, CC-News, central-bank releases and the Reddit Pushshift archive \cite{baumgartner2020pushshift}) are proposed for ingestion through the adapter pattern described in Section~\ref{sec:news-pipeline}. The dictionary baseline is retained as a sanity check on the transformer scorer in the spirit of the methodological caution recommended by \citeonline{lopezdeprado2018advances}. The additional corpora reduce the single-source dependence that a GDELT-only design carries.

\textit{Decision architecture.} The execution layer in \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} is itself deterministic but thin: the XGBoost probability is converted into a directional position by a fixed threshold, and that single-rule layer is evaluated unchanged out of sample. The present work keeps the deterministic-execution commitment but enriches the layer in two directions (Sections~\ref{subsec:late-fusion} and~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution}). First, a small meta-learner combines the calibrated outputs of four sub-models into a single probability rather than relying on a single classifier. Second, a configurable filter chain converts that probability into a trade decision through a sequence of named filters (trend-regime, indicator, pattern, news-context, risk-guard, capital-management, terminal threshold), and each trade carries an audit trail consisting of the contribution of every filter. The architectural commitment is documented in Section~\ref{sec:fusion} as ``agentic perception, deterministic execution''. The divergence is therefore a thicker, multi-filter deterministic execution layer rather than a presence-versus-absence contrast with \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha}.

\textit{Execution path and license.} \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} stops at the backtest stage and reports no live-execution path. The present work runs its backtests on the open-source LEAN engine of QuantConnect, under the Apache 2.0 license, and reuses LEAN's official OANDA brokerage adapter as the eventual live-trading path (Section~\ref{sec:execution-engine}). As recorded in Section~\ref{sec:justification} of the introduction, the motivation is the author's intention to return to live algorithmic trading after the MBA. In this regard, a permissive license and an official brokerage adapter make the path from backtest to paper trading to live trading an exercise in re-targeting the runtime rather than re-implementing the strategy.

The remaining sections of this chapter elaborate the choices summarized above. The empirical comparison against \citeonline{zhang2025macroalpha} on the metrics in common (Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, hit rate, turnover) is reported in Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}.

\section{Research Design} \label{sec:research-design}

The research design is operational rather than theoretical: it specifies what is to be built, on which data, and how the resulting artifact is to be evaluated. The general objective stated in Section~\ref{sec:objectives}, "to develop and evaluate a hybrid algorithmic trading strategy that combines technical, sentiment-based and event-based signals on Forex pairs", decomposes into five concrete deliverables. The empirical evaluation is conducted exclusively on the foreign-exchange market, which is the deliberate scope of this work; the architecture, however, is designed to be asset-class agnostic, so that the market-specific constructs are isolated behind a common instrument abstraction and a set of configurable filters. Adapting the same pipeline to other financial markets (equities, ETFs, bonds, futures, commodities or cryptocurrencies) is therefore, in principle, an exercise in adapter substitution and configuration rather than redesign; this is developed as a direction for future work in Section~\ref{subsec:future-multiasset}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Build a reproducible data pipeline.} Bulk-ingest historical Forex price data and a heterogeneous set of textual corpora into a single canonical storage layer. The pipeline must be re-runnable end-to-end without manual intervention.
    \item \textbf{Construct a feature layer.} Derive technical indicators from price data, sentence-level sentiment scores from financial text, and event-level scores from geopolitical-event corpora. Align all features to a common minute-level grid.
    \item \textbf{Train and backtest a baseline strategy.} Implement a price-only strategy as a control. Evaluate it under a strict walk-forward, purged cross-validation protocol \cite{lopezdeprado2018advances}.
    \item \textbf{Train and backtest a hybrid strategy.} Add the sentiment and event features to the baseline, using a late-fusion architecture (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}), and evaluate it under the same protocol.
    \item \textbf{Report the comparison honestly.} Report Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, hit rate and turnover for both strategies, in-sample and out-of-sample, with explicit discussion of the threats to validity.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the five deliverables as a single data-flow diagram. Heterogeneous data sources (left column) are ingested by self-registering \texttt{NewsDataSource} adapters, normalized into a single Parquet store partitioned by year and month, and converted into the five-family feature space (technical-analysis structural, indicator, pattern, news, market-activity) consumed by five LightGBM sub-models. A logistic meta-learner combines their calibrated outputs into $\hat{p}_t$, and a deterministic chain of filters then converts $\hat{p}_t$ into the trade decision, with every filter contribution persisted in an audit trail. The diagram embodies the methodological commitment of Section~\ref{sec:fusion}, \emph{agentic perception, deterministic execution}: the perception layer (blue dashed box) is allowed to incorporate non-deterministic models because its outputs are precomputed once and read deterministically at backtest time, whereas the execution layer (red dashed box) is rule-based and reproducible regardless of upstream non-determinism. The target evaluation combines LEAN backtests with the combinatorial purged $k$-fold protocol of \citeonline{lopezdeprado2018advances}, and report Sharpe, maximum drawdown, hit rate, average holding time and turnover.

\subsection{Experimental Workflow and Stage Gates} \label{subsec:workflow-stages}

The software pipeline is not the full experimental workflow. In particular, fitting a family model, calibrating the combiner, freezing an artifact, and replaying a strategy are distinct operations even though they share one software package. The following stages define the evidence needed to advance from data acquisition to a reportable result.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Register the experiment.} Record the hypothesis, pair, variants, label, dates, execution assumptions, primary comparison, and candidate-search budget before inspecting evaluation returns. Identify the run as exploratory or confirmatory.
    \item \textbf{Acquire sources.} Preserve provenance and completion records for raw prices and event data. The current GDELT exporter writes partial monthly files while downloading; only its completion marker establishes that the month has finished.
    \item \textbf{Normalize and verify coverage.} Check canonical timestamps, corrupt records, missing days, and overlap among required sources. Record any reduced-window exception separately from the full coverage rule.
    \item \textbf{Prepare features and execution data.} Materialize LEAN prices and lagged event features. Verify warmup, availability at decision time, and feature parity between offline training and engine replay, including the last bar's decision timestamp.
    \item \textbf{Freeze splits and labels.} Define non-overlapping family-fitting, combiner-calibration, and held-out periods. Purge labels crossing split boundaries. Rolling refits, CPCV folds, and embargo intervals require their own schedules and cannot be inferred from one chronological split.
    \item \textbf{Fit family models.} Train each LightGBM sub-model using only fitting-period rows. Keep the common price inputs and dates identical between the baseline and hybrid.
    \item \textbf{Calibrate the combiner.} Fit logistic stacking on the later calibration period using the family models' out-of-sample predictions. Calibration observations remain in-sample for the combined system.
    \item \textbf{Freeze model artifacts.} Save the fitted model, resolved feature configuration, split dates, row counts, seed, input hashes, code revision, and library versions. Record the model hash used by each replay.
    \item \textbf{Check engine and execution assumptions.} Run known-answer controls and inspect costs, position sizing, stops, margin, and leverage. Successful container execution alone does not establish economic realism.
    \item \textbf{Simulate held-out periods.} Load frozen models and replay the same market window under the same execution assumptions. Preserve actual run dates, configuration, decisions, trades, and equity; perform no fitting during replay.
    \item \textbf{Audit and analyze.} Check coverage, decision-to-trade joins, and zero-trade outcomes before interpreting metrics. Report paired effects and uncertainty using methods appropriate to the return series and the candidate-search history.
    \item \textbf{Extend and report.} Evaluate further completed periods with frozen models, or register a new refit that uses only earlier data. Link manuscript tables to run artifacts and disclose missing stages and validity limitations.
\end{enumerate}

Acquisition of later months may continue while earlier completed months are used for training. Inputs to a running experiment must remain stable. A process that has started, a model that has been saved, and a successful replay are different completion states; none alone demonstrates the research hypothesis.

The baseline/hybrid comparison is a paired historical simulation, not a randomized intervention in the market. Both variants must face the same dates and economic assumptions. Minute rows with overlapping fifteen-minute labels are not independent replications, and repeating a daily event value across minutes does not create new independent news observations. A single month provides exploratory evidence about that window, not an estimate of performance across all market regimes. If pilot outcomes guide subsequent model selection, that window becomes development data and must not later be presented as an untouched test set.

The implemented hybrid changes both the news-family input to the combiner and the F4 event gate. Its comparison with the baseline therefore measures their combined effect. Isolating the contribution of either component requires a separately specified ablation.

\noindent\textbf{Type of relationship investigated.}
The present work conducts a \emph{controlled comparison} (ablation) between
two strategy variants on common data and a common protocol. It does not
assert causal mechanisms by which textual signals affect FX rates; it

\section{Data Architecture and Storage} \label{sec:data-architecture}

\textit{Terminology.} This work deliberately adopts the vocabulary of the LEAN execution engine (Section~\ref{sec:execution-engine}) as its system-level standard, precisely because LEAN is the platform on which the empirical work runs: aligning the written methodology with the engine's own concepts removes the translation gap between the dissertation and the running system, and, because LEAN is itself multi-asset, keeps the present Forex study expressible in the same terms as the asset-class-agnostic architecture of Section~\ref{sec:research-design}. Under this convention a \textit{symbol} identifies a tradable instrument, a \textit{security type} names its asset class, a \textit{QuoteBar} is the bid/ask bar at a given \textit{resolution} (tick, minute, daily), and a \textit{market} names the venue. The Forex parallels are stated where helpful: here a symbol is a currency pair (for example EUR/USD), the security type is \textit{forex}, and the price-movement unit is the pip.

The whole experimental pipeline rests on a data-architecture decision: \textit{Apache Parquet is the canonical source of truth for every dataset, regardless of source, while execution-facing derived data is materialized once into a durable engine-native store and reused}. The distinction is deliberate. Some datasets are read a handful of times for analysis, joining and model training; others sit on the hot path of the backtester, which replays the same historical inputs across the many simulations of a parameter and ablation sweep. Forcing both classes into a single format would be a mistake: the cost of the experimental work is not downloading or normalizing once, but executing thousands of backtests, so the dominant optimization target is replay efficiency. The architecture therefore splits storage by reuse rather than imposing one format everywhere. Three properties motivate Parquet as the canonical layer.

First, engine portability. Parquet is a columnar format with mature readers in every language relevant to this work (Python, C\#, Rust, Java). Any future replacement of the backtester, of the model-training stack or of the analysis layer can read the same files without conversion. A backtester-specific format, by contrast, would couple the data architecture to one engine.

Second, queryability. The DuckDB analytical engine reads Parquet directly and executes SQL over the partitioned tree without any ingestion step. Joining sentiment scores to price bars at minute resolution, computing coverage statistics for the news corpora, or producing exploratory plots reduces to a single query rather than a custom loader. The same Parquet tree therefore serves both the backtester and the analytical workflow.

Third, reproducibility. The Parquet tree is the source of truth; every other on-disk or in-memory artifact is derived. If a derived artifact is lost, it is rebuilt from Parquet in seconds. If the Parquet tree itself is lost, it is rebuilt from raw payloads via the ingestion pipeline. There is no ambiguity about which version of which dataset a given backtest used.

The on-disk layout follows a partitioned convention. Forex price data lives at \path{parquet/forex/<pair>/year=YYYY/month=MM/data.parquet}, where \texttt{<pair>} is one of \texttt{eurusd} or \texttt{usdjpy}. News and event data follow the analogous \path{parquet/<source>/<table>/year=YYYY/month=MM/data.parquet} layout already established by the news-ingestion sub-project. Derived sentiment scores live at \path{parquet/sentiment/} and event scores at \path{parquet/events/}, each partitioned in the same way.

The backtester, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:execution-engine}, expects its own resolution-bucketed file format, which it replays on every simulation. Rather than reconvert from Parquet on each run, a recurring cost in CPU and input/output that would be paid thousands of times across a sweep, the present work materializes an engine-native execution store once, at \path{lean-data/}, and reuses it across all runs. This store is durable: it is written to the filesystem, survives between sessions, and is rebuilt only when it is missing or its version is stale. It remains a \textit{derived} artifact (reproducible from the canonical Parquet tree, so Parquet stays the single authoritative copy) but it is not regenerated per run, which is the distinction that matters for replay efficiency. For workstations with spare memory, a copy may optionally be staged on a Linux \texttt{tmpfs} mount at \path{/dev/shm/lean-data/} to serve reads from main memory; that copy is an accelerator re-stageable from the durable store, not the source of truth. Only minute-resolution price and the consolidated decision features are materialized this way. Tick-level data, which would otherwise contribute the bulk of the storage footprint, is kept only in Parquet (approximately five to ten gigabytes for two pairs over ten years) and is consumed during slippage modeling but not during normal backtest runs.

A second decision concerns \textit{when} derived features are computed, and it is what keeps the backtest consistent with eventual live execution. Feature and sentiment computation is a single, mode-agnostic engine (a function of the current bar and the rolling state it carries) run vectorized over history during backtesting and one bar at a time in live trading. A single implementation is non-negotiable: two would expose the deployed model to inputs it was never trained on, the classic train-and-serve skew. Where the execution engine's own indicators must nonetheless be mirrored offline to assemble training data, the mirror is held to numerical parity with the engine by automated tests (Section~\ref{sec:execution-engine}). Its outputs pass through a read-through cache: a request returns the cached value on a hit and otherwise computes it, stores it, and returns it. The consequence is that every bar is computed \textit{exactly once}, the first time it exists, and every later access is a cache hit. During a backtest sweep the first run fills the cache and the remaining runs (which vary only the filters, thresholds and ablations over the same history) read from it, eliminating the repeated recomputation that dominated the naive design. In live execution the same engine and the same cache are used: warm-up history and post-restart state are cache hits, and the only genuine computation is the bar that has just closed, because it did not exist before. The model itself is always trained offline on the historical panel and loaded for inference; production never trains. The cache backend is left open behind a port abstraction: an in-process least-recently-used structure and a file-backed store suffice for the present work, with a higher-performance backend available as a later, optional upgrade without any change to the calling code.

\section{Forex Price-Data Pipeline} \label{sec:price-pipeline}

Historical price data for EUR/USD and USD/JPY is to be obtained from Dukascopy\footnote{\url{https://www.dukascopy.com/}}, a Swiss broker that publishes free tick-level historical data for major Forex pairs back to 2003 through a public HTTP datafeed. Retrieval is a direct HTTP download from that datafeed: each hour of tick data is a compressed binary file fetched over HTTP, requiring no account, no credentials and no third-party client library. The choice of Dukascopy over the available alternatives reflects the constraints of the present work: the data must cover the full target window (2015-02 to 2024-12), it must include both pairs, it must be free for academic use, and bulk download must be possible without rate limits prohibitive to a ten-year retrieval. HistData covers the window but only at minute resolution; the OANDA v20 application programming interface offers approximately five years of rolling history; Interactive Brokers is paid and rate-limited; TrueFX has a smaller pair set. Dukascopy is the only option that satisfies all four constraints. As a resilience measure, a lightweight daily-resolution fallback adapter (Yahoo Finance via the \texttt{yfinance} package) is retained so that, should the Dukascopy datafeed be unavailable for a given month, the pipeline continues at reduced fidelity rather than stalling; the fallback is not the primary backtest input.

The choice to commit to a single venue, rather than a synthetic best-bid/best-ask composite across multiple ECNs, follows \citeonline{hallsmoore2015} (Ch.~3, §3.3.3, p.~19): retail Forex brokers are not obligated to share trade prices with each other or with central aggregators, so any composite feed reconstructed from public sources differs from the executable price available at a given broker. The present work therefore commits to the Dukascopy bid/ask stream throughout the pipeline; the transaction-cost model of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} is calibrated against the same venue's realized-spread distribution, so that backtest costs and live-execution costs are sourced from a consistent quote stream.

A second constraint specific to FX is that the total return of a multi-day position includes the rollover swap differential, not only the directional price move. \citeonline{chan2013algo} (Ch.~5, eq.~5.6, pp.~113--114) makes this explicit: ignoring rollover on overnight FX positions is equivalent to a look-ahead error of opposite sign to the rate differential, and the magnitude can dominate the daily directional return on low-volatility pairs. The present work therefore models rollover as part of the daily P\&L: for any position held across the New York 17:00 ET roll, the carry contribution is computed from the broker-published swap quote for the pair on that date, with the standard convention that the pair-side rate differential determines the sign. The convention is recorded in the unit tests of the backtester and is identical for the baseline and for the hybrid strategy, so that the comparison reports the marginal contribution of textual features net of identical carry treatment.

The price-ingestion code is planned as a sibling project to the existing news-ingestion code, reusing the same self-registering adapter pattern. A single market-data abstraction defines four operations (\texttt{plan}, \texttt{is\_done}, \texttt{execute}, \texttt{unit\_key}) and each concrete source (Dukascopy first, with HistData and TrueFX available as cross-checks) is to register an implementation under a stable name. The unit of work is one (pair, year, month) tuple, as in the news pipeline; idempotency is guaranteed by inspecting Parquet file existence on disk. The implementation status of the price-ingestion sibling at the time of submission is reported in Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}.

Each hour of ticks is downloaded over HTTP as a compressed binary blob from the Dukascopy data servers and written first, unmodified, as a raw payload under \path{raw/dukascopy/...}; decoding is deferred to the transform stage, which keeps fetching and normalizing as separate, separately re-runnable steps. The transform stage then decompresses and decodes the blobs into bid/ask quote records, writes them as Parquet under the canonical layout, and resamples them into minute-resolution \texttt{QuoteBar} records (open, high, low, close on both bid and ask, plus tick count) at \path{parquet/forex/<pair>/...} alongside the tick partition. The minute-resolution Parquet is the input to all downstream analysis and feature engineering, and the source from which the durable \path{lean-data/} execution store is materialized once for replay (Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture}). Figure~\ref{fig:data-pipeline} shows this price path end to end.

\subsection*{i.i.d.\ assumption} \label{subsec:iid}
Minute-resolution Forex returns are \textbf{not} independent and
identically distributed. They exhibit serial autocorrelation in absolute
returns (volatility clustering), regime-dependent unconditional means
during macroeconomic-announcement windows, and a fat-tailed conditional
distribution that varies across the session. Naive $k$-fold
cross-validation, which presumes exchangeability of training and test
samples, leaks future information into the past and inflates in-sample
metrics. This work mitigates the violation in two ways: by adopting the
combinatorial purged $k$-fold scheme with an embargo period
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), which removes feature observations whose
forward label horizon overlaps the validation fold, and by walk-forward
training, which preserves the chronological order of the data at every
refit. Neither mitigation makes the data i.i.d.; together they avoid the
specific failure modes that the i.i.d.\ assumption would create.

\section{News and Event-Data Pipeline} \label{sec:news-pipeline}

The news side of the pipeline ingests several corpora, each as an independent self-registering adapter. The order in which adapters are built reflects an explicit relevance ranking for Forex.

\subsection{Source priority and rationale} \label{subsec:news-priority}

The Forex literature reviewed in Sections~\ref{sec:fx-market} and~\ref{sec:geopolitical} attributes the largest cross-rate moves to monetary-policy communication and to broad geopolitical shocks. The corpora are therefore prioritized by their proximity to these two drivers.

\textbf{Central-bank releases} (FOMC, ECB and BOJ) come first. The volume is small but the signal-to-noise ratio is the highest of any corpus available: a single sentence in an FOMC statement routinely moves USD/JPY by tens of pips. The planned adapters scrape the official archives directly and store one document per release. Implementation status at the time of submission is reported in Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}.

\textbf{GDELT 2.0} \cite{leetaru2013gdelt} provides the broadest coverage of the target window through two of its public tables, Events and the Global Knowledge Graph (GKG), both updated every fifteen minutes. Both tables are also available as public Google BigQuery datasets (\texttt{gdelt-bq.gdeltv2.events}, \texttt{gdelt-bq.gdeltv2.gkg}), free to query under GDELT's open-data terms. Rather than the per-fifteen-minute HTTP file pull GDELT otherwise requires (roughly 350{,}000 requests for the target window), the present work materializes the target window \textit{once} into a private BigQuery table under the project's own billing (\texttt{CREATE TABLE ... AS SELECT * FROM gdelt-bq.gdeltv2.events/gkg WHERE <date> BETWEEN ...}), after which every further read is a cheap query against that private copy rather than a re-scan of the public source. Events and GKG are joined on \texttt{SOURCEURL = DocumentIdentifier} (a shared source-article URL), one calendar day at a time, each day's result written to its own Parquet partition. The exact acquisition and join queries are version-controlled at \path{algo-suite/scripts/bigquery/} for reproducibility.

The \textbf{Geopolitical Risk index} of \citeonline{caldara2022geopolitical} is downloaded as a single CSV from the authors' public site\footnote{\url{https://www.matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm}}. It is a derived index rather than a raw corpus, but its inclusion is methodologically important: GPR provides a continuous regime measure that complements the discrete event records of GDELT.

The \textbf{FNSPID} corpus of \citeonline{dong2024fnspid} is a recent large-scale financial-news dataset combining 29.7 million stock-price records with 15.7 million time-aligned news articles spanning S\&P 500 companies from 1999 to 2023. Its bias is towards US equities, but the macroeconomic coverage it contains (Federal Reserve commentary, oil and dollar shocks, geopolitical reactions) carries information for USD-denominated pairs. It is ingested via the Hugging Face datasets interface as a one-shot bulk pull.

The \textbf{Common Crawl News} dataset (CC-News)\footnote{Common Crawl Foundation. \url{https://commoncrawl.org/blog/news-dataset-available}.} provides general-news coverage from August 2016 onwards. It adds breadth at the cost of a higher noise floor, since most articles in any given month are unrelated to Forex; relevance filtering is therefore part of the feature-extraction step rather than the ingestion step.

The \textbf{Reddit archive} is consumed via the Pushshift dumps documented by \citeonline{baumgartner2020pushshift}. Three subreddits are retained for relevance (\texttt{r/Forex}, \texttt{r/economics} and \texttt{r/wallstreetbets}) covering the period 2008 to 2023.

Two further sources are listed as nice-to-have additions, scoped for inclusion only if the build budget allows. The \textbf{Trump Twitter Archive}\footnote{\url{https://www.thetrumparchive.com/}} provides every public tweet from the accounts of Donald J.~Trump between 2009 and 2021 in structured form. It serves as a single-influencer corpus for the 2017--2021 period, during which Trump's tweets demonstrably moved USD/CNY, USD/JPY and EUR/USD on multiple occasions through tariff threats, criticism of the Federal Reserve and trade-negotiation announcements. \textbf{StockTwits}\footnote{\url{https://stocktwits.com/}} provides retail-trader chatter with a finance-domain bias and is accessed via its public stream.

\subsection{Excluded sources} \label{subsec:news-excluded}

Live access to Twitter/X is excluded. Following the platform's policy changes from 2023 onwards, the API tiers compatible with bulk historical retrieval moved out of the budget range realistic for an academic project, and unauthenticated scraping is both technically blocked and prohibited by the platform's terms of service. The present work treats this as a documented scoping decision rather than a methodological gap; the social-media tier is supplied by the Reddit archive, by StockTwits and, for the 2017--2021 sub-period, by the Trump Twitter Archive.

\subsection{Coverage rule for the training window} \label{subsec:coverage-rule}

The target time window is 2015-02-19 to 2024-12-31, ten years, with the lower bound fixed by the GDELT 2.0 launch date. Within this window the actual training span is determined empirically by a coverage rule: the training window is the largest contiguous span over which at least two news corpora are active for at least eighty per cent of the constituent months. The eighty-per-cent figure is an experimental threshold, not a value derived from prior literature: it is a working choice for the present pass, expected to be revisited against the actual coverage matrix once ingestion completes, and adjusted if a different cut-off yields a materially better-supported training window. The rule is computed from the ingested data after the first phase of the pipeline finishes, and the resulting coverage matrix (corpora $\times$ month) is reported as a figure in the experimental chapter. The rule replaces an arbitrary ex-ante cut-off with a reproducible criterion that survives the eventual addition or removal of a source.

\section{Feature Layer} \label{sec:feature-layer}

The feature layer maps raw price and text data into the input matrix consumed by the strategy model. The decomposition adopted here follows the five-family input space used by a discretionary Forex trader: technical-analysis features describing market structure, indicator features quantifying momentum and volatility, chart and candlestick pattern features triggering entries, news features supplying context and risk vetoes, and market-activity features capturing the relative trading activity of each currency. The first three families and the market-activity family are derived from the price Parquet; the news family is derived from the text and event corpora and is itself decomposed into a sentiment sub-family and an event sub-family. Six subsections describe the resulting features.

\subsection{Technical-analysis features} \label{subsec:ta-structural-features}

Technical-analysis features describe the structural state of the market at a given timestamp: whether the price is in an uptrend, a downtrend or a range; where the recent supports and resistances lie; and where the most recent swing highs and lows sit \cite{murphy1999technical}. Three groups of features are produced. \textit{Trend-regime} features classify each bar as bullish, bearish or sideways using the Average Directional Index (ADX) together with the slope of a long-period exponential moving average; the regime label optionally gates the rule layer described in Section~\ref{sec:fusion}. \textit{Support-and-resistance} features track rolling extrema and pivot points: the highest high and lowest low over a configurable window, the previous-day pivot point and its derived support and resistance levels, and the distance of the current price to each of these levels in pip units. \textit{Swing-structure} features identify swing highs and swing lows from the price series via a ZigZag-style detector, exposing the location and magnitude of the last few swings; they form the input to the chart-pattern detection of Section~\ref{subsec:pattern-features}.

The trend-regime perception of this family is computed on Heikin-Ashi bars rather than on raw candles. Heikin-Ashi (Japanese for ``average bar'') is a public charting transform from the Japanese candlestick tradition, with no single credited inventor: each bar's open is the mean of the previous bar's open and close, its close the mean of the current open, high, low and close, and its high and low the extremes of those values and the raw bar. The construction absorbs single-bar noise so that a trend appears as a run of same-coloured bars with small opposite shadows; it was introduced to Western technical analysis by \citeonline{valcu2004heikinashi} and is a standard chart type in charting platforms. The implementation smooths the transformed series a second time (a double-smoothed Heikin-Ashi) before reading the regime direction, and the risk and exit template used by the H4 and H1 experiments of Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} is named after it because its entry logic belongs to the same family.

\subsection{Indicator features} \label{subsec:indicator-features}

Indicator features quantify momentum, volatility and microstructure conditions, building on the standard families surveyed in \cite{murphy1999technical}: trend-following indicators (simple and exponential moving averages, MACD), momentum oscillators (RSI, stochastic oscillator), volatility filters (ATR, rolling realized volatility, Bollinger bandwidth) and microstructure proxies derived from tick data (rolling tick count, average bid--ask spread). Indicator periods follow the conventions familiar to the author from his prior retail Forex trading practice and are fixed before any backtesting, so that hyperparameter optimization does not silently extend over the indicator family. All indicator features are computed with strict point-in-time discipline: at time $t$, only data with timestamp $\leq t$ enters the feature value, and any rolling-window statistic uses only the trailing window. Indicator warm-up is treated in the same spirit: no decision is taken, and no training row is emitted, before every indicator that the features read has accumulated its full warm-up history, so that the model is never trained on, nor asked to score, a partially initialized indicator state.

\subsection{Chart and candlestick pattern features} \label{subsec:pattern-features}

Pattern features encode the graphical-analysis tradition of technical analysis: discrete shapes formed by recent price action that traders have historically associated with directional bias. Two strands are implemented. The first is the Japanese candlestick tradition codified in the West by \citeonline{nison1991candlestick}; the second is the classical chart-pattern tradition cataloged in \cite{bulkowski2005encyclopedia} and given a computational footing by \citeonline{lo2000foundations}.

Candlestick patterns are deterministic functions of one to five consecutive OHLC bars: the Doji, the Hammer, the Engulfing pattern, the Morning and Evening Stars, the Three White Soldiers and the Three Black Crows, among approximately sixty patterns enumerated in \cite{nison1991candlestick}. The implementation reuses the reference detectors of the open-source TA-Lib library, which exposes one function per pattern (e.g., \texttt{CDLDOJI}, \texttt{CDLENGULFING}, \texttt{CDLHAMMER}). Each detector returns, for every bar, an integer-valued indicator: zero if no pattern is present, a positive value if a bullish instance is detected, a negative value if a bearish instance is detected. The encoding lets a downstream model treat each pattern as a categorical-with-direction feature without further preprocessing.

Classical chart patterns operate on longer time scales than candlesticks and require the swing-structure features of Section~\ref{subsec:ta-structural-features} as input. Patterns considered include head-and-shoulders, double tops and double bottoms, ascending and descending triangles, and bull and bear flags. The detection procedure follows \citeonline{lo2000foundations}: the price series is first smoothed by kernel regression to identify local extrema, the resulting sequence of extrema is matched against parametric pattern templates, and a confidence score is computed from the residual between the observed sequence and the matched template. The pattern catalog and the failure-rate statistics reported by \citeonline{bulkowski2005encyclopedia} inform the prior on each pattern's reliability and the conservative thresholds used to admit a detection into the feature stream. As with all features in this chapter, pattern detections are emitted only at the bar that closes the pattern, so that no future information enters the feature value.

\subsection{Sentiment features} \label{subsec:sentiment-features}

Sentiment features are produced by two parallel scorers applied to the same news input, in line with the discussion in Section~\ref{subsec:fin-nlp}. The first is a dictionary-based baseline using the Loughran--McDonald financial-domain lexicon \cite{loughran2011liability}. The second is a transformer-based scorer using the FinBERT family \cite{araci2019finbert,yang2020finbert}; both FinBERT variants are evaluated and the better-performing of the two on a small in-domain validation set is retained. The transformer scorer takes a tokenized sentence as input and returns a scalar polarity in $[-1, +1]$ together with a confidence score; sentence-level scores are aggregated to article level by length-weighted averaging and to time-bucket level by a confidence-weighted mean over articles in the bucket. Time buckets are aligned to the same minute grid as the price features.

Sentiment is attributed \textit{per currency} rather than per pair. Each article is mapped to the currencies it concerns by combining the currency mentioned with its issuing authority or jurisdiction (ECB and the euro area $\to$ EUR; the Federal Reserve and the United States $\to$ USD; the Bank of Japan and Japan $\to$ JPY), so that a single article may contribute to more than one currency stream and a multi-currency headline with opposing tone is attributed with reduced confidence. The pair-level sentiment feature is then formed as the base-minus-quote polarity differential of the two currency streams ($\mathrm{EUR/USD}$ from the EUR and USD streams, $\mathrm{USD/JPY}$ from the USD and JPY streams) so that a US-macro release (for example an FOMC decision) propagates to both pairs through the shared USD stream. This mirrors the structure of an exchange rate as the relative valuation of two currencies, in the same spirit as the interest-rate differential of the carry trade, and is the textual analog of the cross-sectional, exposure-based treatment of geopolitical risk discussed in Section~\ref{sec:geopolitical}.

The choice to preserve minute-level arrival timing on the sentiment side is not arbitrary. \citeonline{hafezxie2013intraday} report that sentiment inflection points extracted from macro news predict intraday EUR/USD moves for several hours after the inflection point, which is exactly the type of temporal structure that would be destroyed by collapsing article arrivals into a daily index before the backtest. The present work therefore preserves article timestamps and only aggregates to the minute bucket required by the price grid.

The dictionary baseline is retained even after the transformer scorer is operational. \citeonline{tetlock2007giving} and \citeonline{loughran2011liability} establish that domain-specific dictionaries already provide measurable predictive content in financial settings; the comparison between the dictionary baseline and the transformer scorer therefore acts as a sanity check for the larger model, in line with the methodological caution recommended by \citeonline{lopezdeprado2018advances}. Where the inference-latency budget of a future live deployment is binding, the comparison also documents the cost--benefit trade-off between the cheaper lexicon scorer and the more expensive transformer scorer (Section~\ref{subsec:llms}). Whether to escalate to a larger foundation model in the FinGPT \cite{liu2023fingpt} or BloombergGPT \cite{wu2023bloomberggpt} class is treated as an open question in Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility} rather than asserted in advance.

Recent LLM-based results in financial sentiment analysis \cite{fatouros2023chatgpt,ballinari2025fxllm,iacovides2025findpo} motivate that future scorer swap, but they do not justify skipping the FinBERT-class baseline in the present dissertation. Their role here is methodological inspiration for a later perception-layer upgrade, not evidence that the current implementation should bypass the conservative baseline / challenger comparison.

\subsection{Event features} \label{subsec:event-features}

Event features are derived from GDELT and from the GPR index. From the GDELT Events table the daily counts of CAMEO event categories relevant to Forex (monetary-policy actions, sanctions, military activity, sovereign downgrades) are extracted, together with their average tone and average Goldstein score. From the GDELT Global Knowledge Graph, daily aggregates of the geopolitical-actor co-mention graph centered on the United States, the euro area and Japan are computed. The GPR index of \citeonline{caldara2022geopolitical} is incorporated as the daily-frequency continuous regime feature. All event features are produced at daily resolution and forward-filled onto the minute grid; the asymmetry in resolution is intentional and reflects the asymmetry in the underlying signal. The first GDELT aggregate implemented is a daily \textit{event intensity}, the unweighted mean Goldstein score of the day's events. Because a daily aggregate summarizes the whole UTC day, it is only knowable once that day has ended: the forward fill therefore applies a one-day publication lag, so that the aggregate of day $D$ becomes visible from 00:00~UTC of day $D+1$ and every minute of day $D$ carries the aggregate of day $D-1$. Exposing the aggregate of day $D$ during day $D$ itself would leak up to twenty-four hours of future information into every bar that reads it (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).

\subsection{Why a coded event index rather than text inference} \label{subsec:why-event-index}

The news signal that reaches the filter chain in the experiments of Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} is the GDELT event intensity, the mean Goldstein score of the day's coded events, and not a sentiment score inferred by the dissertation's own model over article text. The decisive reason is time, and the story is worth telling because it shaped the data architecture. The first ingestion path pulled GDELT's fifteen-minute update files over HTTP, one request per unit, through the download tool of Section~\ref{sec:news-pipeline}. For the window of interest that meant about 350,000 units; GDELT's servers answer bulk pulls with \texttt{429 Too Many Requests}, so the tool had to back off and respect a politeness interval, which put the floor on a single pass at more than nineteen hours before any retry, with the retries themselves throttled. Two attempts stalled on that wall, and a further night was lost when the writer that persisted the downloaded units to the network-attached storage failed part-way through on a transient network error and left the partition inconsistent; the data root was moved to the workstation's local NVMe drive after that, a change made for reliability rather than speed: it removed the network as a failure point in a job that had to run unattended for days, and, as expected, changed the throughput little, since I/O had never been the bottleneck: the request rate was. The path was superseded on September 25, 2026 by a bulk materialization through Google BigQuery: a single \texttt{CREATE TABLE AS SELECT} over the public \path{gdelt-bq.gdeltv2.events} table, exported natively to Parquet, replaced hundreds of thousands of HTTP requests with one scan. Even so, the events table alone took about twenty-six hours of wall time to land nineteen months (2015-02 to 2017-02, nine to ten million rows a month), and the ingestion was still running while the experiments were executed, month by month, with each month entering the protocol only once its completion marker existed. The article text needed for a sentiment model lives in the Global Knowledge Graph, a 22-terabyte unpartitioned table with no article bodies, only quotations and metadata, whose materialization would have cost a multiple of that time and budget. Within the submission schedule the choice was between an event index available for the whole window and a text index available for none of it.

Beyond the schedule, five reasons make the event index a defensible primary signal rather than a stopgap, and the reader should weigh them against the cost, which is that the text half of the news family (Section~\ref{subsec:sentiment-features}) stays empty in the reported runs.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The text is not in the corpus.} GDELT 2.0 distributes coded events and, in the Global Knowledge Graph, metadata about the articles (themes, tone, quotations), not the articles themselves \cite{leetaru2013gdelt}. Recovering bodies for 2015--2017 would mean fetching source URLs, most of which no longer resolve, under terms of use that forbid bulk retrieval, or licensing a news archive outside the budget of this work (the same scoping rule applied to the social-media tier in Section~\ref{subsec:news-excluded}). The quotation field of the Knowledge Graph is the one text source that could be scored without fetching, and it is left as the registered route for the text half.
    \item \textbf{Point-in-time validity.} The Goldstein scale is a fixed, published mapping from CAMEO event codes to a conflict--cooperation value \cite{goldstein1992scale}; its value for a 2016 event is the same today as it was on the day. A transformer sentiment model, by contrast, is trained on corpora that include the evaluation years: a FinBERT-class checkpoint \cite{araci2019finbert} or a 2024 encoder has read 2015--2017 news during pre-training, so its scores are not what a trader could have computed at the time, and the leakage cannot be bounded. For a backtest whose whole argument rests on untouched windows, a vintage-safe feature is worth more than a richer one.
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility and audit.} The index is a deterministic aggregate of a public table: every value in every run can be recomputed from the recorded month hashes, with no model weights, GPU, tokenizer or library version in the path. The provenance discipline of Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution} extends to the news input without exception.
    \item \textbf{Scale and coverage.} The window holds nine to ten million events per month in more than a hundred languages; aggregating them takes minutes, whereas scoring even the quotation subset with a large encoder is days of GPU time per year, and an English-language, corporate-news classifier trained on the Financial PhraseBank would cover a fraction of a corpus whose subject is geopolitical, not corporate.
    \item \textbf{Construct fit.} The hypothesis concerns geopolitical event risk, and event-coded intensity is that construct measured directly; tone inferred from prose is a proxy for it. Keeping the sentiment threshold in F4 as a declared but unfilled parameter preserves the design slot, so that adding text later is a data change registered as its own experiment, not a change to the chain.
\end{enumerate}

The cost is stated where it bites: the hybrid differs from the baseline by one daily scalar repeated across the day's bars, and Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} shows that this scalar carries no hourly directional information the money-management layer can use. Whether article text would, remains an open question that this dissertation frames but does not answer.

\subsection{Market-activity features} \label{subsec:volume-features}

The fifth family captures the relative trading activity of each currency. The foreign-exchange market is over-the-counter and has no consolidated traded volume; the present work therefore uses \textit{tick volume} (the count of price updates per interval, the standard activity proxy in retail Forex platforms) as recorded in the QuoteBar features of Section~\ref{sec:price-pipeline}. Per-bar tick volume is aggregated across the configured pairs into a daily \textit{currency-strength} signal per currency: the share of total activity attributed to EUR, USD and JPY, together with the day's activity ranking. The premise, drawn from the discretionary trader's heuristic, is that the currency concentrating the day's activity tends to indicate the direction in which the market is leaning. Because the signal is cross-pair (it cannot be computed from a single pair in isolation) it is materialized at this layer rather than inside the per-pair execution engine. The feature is deliberately universe-aware: with the two pairs of the present work it covers EUR, USD and JPY only partially (USD being shared), and it strengthens as the pair universe is extended toward the major pairs in future work. Like the other price-derived families, it feeds a dedicated context filter in the deterministic chain of Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution}.

\section{Fusion and Deterministic Execution} \label{sec:fusion}

The strategy combines the five feature families of Section~\ref{sec:feature-layer} through a two-phase pipeline. The first phase performs late fusion of the perception layer; the second phase maps the fused output to a trade decision through a deterministic rule implemented as a configurable chain of \textit{filters}. The two phases are designed separately because the methodological commitment of this work hinges on keeping the second phase rule-based even when the first phase incorporates non-deterministic components such as transformer-based language models.

\subsection{Phase one: late fusion of the perception layer} \label{subsec:late-fusion}

% filter set (Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution}, F1-F7) are the full delivery
% target. The implementation is built in stages (see "Phased delivery" at the end of this

Five sub-models are trained independently, in line with the late-fusion discussion in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}. The first four cover the price-derived families (technical-analysis structural features, indicator features, chart-and-candlestick pattern features, and market-activity features). The fifth covers the news-derived family, in which sentiment and event features are concatenated into a single text-derived input matrix. Each sub-model produces a calibrated probability of an upward move at horizon $H$. The training label is a fixed-horizon binary label: it equals 1 if the mid price (the midpoint of the bid and ask closes) fifteen minute bars after the decision bar is strictly higher than at the decision bar, and 0 otherwise, so that $H$ is fifteen minutes in the present configuration. A small meta-learner (logistic regression with a single regularization hyperparameter) combines the five probabilities into a single calibrated output $\hat{p}_t \in [0,1]$.

Late-fusion is the safer default when feature families have different signal-to-noise ratios, as is the case here between price-derived features (low noise, high frequency) and text-derived features (high noise, lower effective frequency). Training all features in a single early-fusion model risks the high-frequency price features dominating the loss surface during gradient descent and squeezing the textual features out of the learned representation.

The choice of sub-model architecture is gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM) for each family, on the basis that the feature spaces are mixed-type, moderately sized, and well within the regime where boosting outperforms or matches deep models on tabular finance data. A switch to a small Transformer encoder over the time axis is reserved as future work and is not part of the deliverable for this dissertation.

Two strategy variants are trained on the same data splits: a \textit{baseline} that uses only the four price-derived sub-models (technical-analysis structural, indicator, pattern and market-activity), and a \textit{hybrid} that adds the news-derived sub-model. The two variants share an identical meta-learner specification; the only difference is whether the news-family probability enters the meta-learner's input. This isolates the marginal contribution of the textual features.

\subsubsection{Phased delivery from a minimum viable baseline} \label{subsubsec:phased-delivery}

The architecture above describes the full delivery target. Implementation proceeds in stages, and each stage is itself tradable, so that earlier stages can be empirically validated before complexity is added. The exact family decomposition, the indicator set within each family, and the filter set retained at each stage are deliberately treated as subject to revision based on ablation results: each stage is a checkpoint whose components must demonstrably contribute before the next stage is built on top of it.

The \textbf{minimum viable baseline} (Stage 1) covers exactly the components a Forex strategy needs to function and to protect capital: a single price-derived feature set, one LightGBM sub-model producing a calibrated directional probability, a threshold-based entry and exit rule on that probability, fixed-fraction position sizing, an ATR-based stop-loss, and a portfolio-level drawdown cap. This stage proves the data-and-execution pipeline end to end and produces a strategy that can be evaluated for capital growth before any added complexity is justified. Stage 2 splits the price features into per-family sub-models (technical-analysis structural, indicator, pattern, market-activity) and introduces the logistic meta-learner of the previous paragraphs. Stage 3 adds the news-derived sub-model (FinBERT-class sentiment, GDELT event features and the GPR index), defining the hybrid variant of Section~\ref{subsec:late-fusion}. Stage 4 introduces the configurable filter chain of Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution} (trend-regime, news-context, risk-guard, capital-management and terminal-threshold filters) on top of the meta-learner output. The progression is monotone in expressiveness: each stage retains the entry-exit-sizing-protection nucleus of Stage 1 and adds optional channels and gates above it.

\subsection{Phase two: deterministic execution as a filter chain} \label{subsec:deterministic-execution}

The trading decision is produced by a configurable chain of deterministic \textit{filters} applied in sequence to the running state. The pattern is borrowed from the layered request-processing model common in Java enterprise frameworks and adopts the FX-industry term \textit{filter} to name the chain elements. Each filter receives the accumulated state, contributes a recommendation in \{BUY, SELL, HOLD, NEUTRAL, ABSTAIN\} together with a textual reason, and may either pass to the next filter or veto by short-circuiting the chain to HOLD. The complete sequence of filter contributions is persisted as the audit trail for every simulated trade, so each backtest entry is explainable down to the contribution of each filter. Concretely, every decision the chain emits is written to a bar-level decision table (\texttt{decisions.parquet}), each row carrying the identifier of the trade open at that instant in the execution engine's own trade ledger; the ledger groups fills flat-to-flat, so that one trade corresponds to one position episode, from flat to flat, and every decision can be joined to the trade it opened, managed or closed.

The implemented core is F1--F7. The broader design additionally proposes a market-activity filter, giving eight roles: a trend-regime filter (Section~\ref{subsec:ta-structural-features}), an indicator filter (Section~\ref{subsec:indicator-features}), a chart-and-candlestick pattern filter (Section~\ref{subsec:pattern-features}), a market-activity filter that reads the daily currency-strength signal (Section~\ref{subsec:volume-features}) and biases the direction toward the day's dominant currency, a news-context filter that consumes the sentiment and event features (Sections~\ref{subsec:sentiment-features}--\ref{subsec:event-features}) and vetoes on active high-risk events, a risk-guard filter (drawdown caps, leverage cap, portfolio-at-risk cap), a capital-management filter that computes the lot size and verifies margin, and a terminal threshold-rule filter that aggregates the accumulated recommendations and emits the final decision. The terminal filter applies the rule
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{decision}_t = \begin{cases}
\text{BUY}  & \text{if } \hat{p}_t > \theta_{\text{high}} \text{ and } v_t = 0 \text{ and } (\neg g \lor r_t = \text{bull}), \\
\text{SELL} & \text{if } \hat{p}_t < \theta_{\text{low}} \text{ and } v_t = 0 \text{ and } (\neg g \lor r_t = \text{bear}), \\
\text{HOLD} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where $\hat{p}_t$ is the meta-learner's probability output from Section~\ref{subsec:late-fusion}, $r_t$ is the trend regime from the trend-regime filter, $v_t$ is the event-veto flag from the news-context filter, and $g$ is the strategy's regime-gate switch. With $g$ on, the rule is the trend-following form in which the regime must agree with the direction the probability points to; with $g$ off, the decision depends on $\hat{p}_t$ alone and the trend-regime filter contributes only its own veto on a timeframe conflict. The thresholds $\theta_{\text{high}}$ and $\theta_{\text{low}}$ are calibrated on the validation span and held fixed during the test span; they are not learned by the model. Every parameter of every filter --- the thresholds and $g$, the risk-guard caps, the sizing economics and the news-context thresholds --- is declared in the strategy's configuration file rather than in code, so a strategy variant differs from its base by a configuration diff and the resolved configuration is written next to every simulation's results. The first pilot (Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}) runs with $g$ off; the reason is documented with that pilot's evidence. Capital management and order execution on top of the decision (position sizing, partial-take-profit ladders, trailing-stop logic, exposure caps per pair) follow a rule-based scheme grounded in standard retail Forex algorithmic-trading practice. The implementation planned for this work is a clean-room rewrite in Python, written from first principles and without reference to any prior third-party codebase, as described in Section~\ref{sec:execution-engine}.

Figure~\ref{fig:filter-chain} makes explicit a distinction that the prose description alone can obscure. The chain is drawn in layers organized by function rather than by exact implementation order, and not every filter plays the same role. The top layer contains context and confirmation filters that accumulate directional or situational evidence from price structure, indicators, patterns and news context; the middle layer contains hard veto gates on execution viability, risk and capital constraints, any one of which short-circuits the chain to HOLD; the bottom layer is the terminal rule that maps the surviving state to BUY, SELL or HOLD. The nodes marked $\cdots$ indicate that the chain is extensible and may include additional filters not shown explicitly: some gates in the diagram are illustrative and need not all be present in the first implementation. The purpose of the figure is to make explicit the distinction between evidence-contributing filters and veto-capable execution gates.

The chain is configurable: the order, presence and parameters of each filter live in a YAML configuration file rather than in the engine code, so adding, removing or reordering filters in subsequent strategy variants requires no engine changes. This is the structural device that makes the future-work tiers of Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusion} feasible without re-architecting the backtester: an additional perception agent or a consensus aggregator slots in as another filter in the chain.

The \textit{order} of the filters is deliberately treated as a configurable design choice rather than a fixed assumption. Because each filter acts on the state accumulated by the filters before it, two orderings of the same set of filters can produce different decisions, and there is no a priori reason to commit to one ordering in advance. The present work therefore determines the filter ordering empirically: candidate orderings, together with the presence or absence of individual filters, are evaluated by simulation, and the configuration that performs best is retained. To keep this search consistent with the validation protocol of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, the ordering is treated as a hyperparameter of the execution layer rather than as a free parameter of the final evaluation. It is selected on the training and validation folds of the combinatorial purged $k$-fold scheme, and the chosen ordering is frozen before the single held-out test pass. The flexibility is thus a search space explored only on in-sample data, never a degree of freedom exercised on the test span, so it cannot inflate the reported out-of-sample result. The comparison between competing orderings is reported as part of the ablation study in Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).

The same treatment applies to the \textit{timeframe} on which each filter operates. The strategy is multi-timeframe: a decision is taken on the finest bar of the trading grid, but an individual filter may analyze a coarser timeframe to contribute its evidence about that decision, in the manner of a discretionary trader who reads a higher timeframe for context and a lower one for entry. A trend-regime filter may therefore evaluate, say, the four-hour bar while the entry decision is made on the one-minute bar. The supported timeframes are the standard Forex set (one, five, fifteen and thirty minutes; one and four hours; one day), and each filter declares the timeframe it reads in the same configuration file as the chain order. Because every reusable bar series is precomputed once by the transformation stage of Section~\ref{sec:price-pipeline} and read through a cache (Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture}), making a filter observe a different timeframe costs a lookup, not a recomputation: the canonical Parquet store holds each timeframe, materialized ahead of the simulation sweep, and any timeframe not yet present is computed once on first request and reused thereafter. As with the chain order, the per-filter timeframe is a hyperparameter selected on the training and validation folds and frozen before the held-out test pass; the choice of which timeframe each layer reads is therefore part of the same ablation, not a free parameter of the final evaluation.

\subsection{Extended filter set: perception and execution quality gates} \label{subsec:extended-filters}

The filter chain described above (F1--F7) forms the baseline. The review of prior monographs in Chapter~\ref{cap:theoretical-foundation} suggests seven additional filters that can be configured into the chain without structural changes. Each one is motivated by a specific gap or methodological caution identified in the related work.

\paragraph{F8: Candlestick Confirmation Gate (Felix, 2024).}
Blocks a trade if no confirmatory candlestick pattern appears in the trailing $k$ bars. The motivation comes from \citeonline{felix2024swing}, which explicitly encodes candlestick patterns as input variables in an ML swing-trading study. The present filter is therefore an adaptation of that modeling choice, not a direct reproduction of Felix's full feature set: it uses a deterministic confirmation gate before the risk layers. The initial catalog prioritizes patterns explicitly discussed in the monograph (Hammer, Shooting Star, Bullish/Bearish Engulfing, Harami, Doji, Inverted Hammer, Three Black Crows and Morning Star) and can later be extended through the TA-Lib library (\texttt{CDL*} functions), which provides standardized, deterministic pattern recognition based on the \citeonline{nison1991candlestick} and \citeonline{bulkowski2005encyclopedia} specifications. Parameters: $k=5$ (configurable), pattern list (expandable up to $\sim$60 TA-Lib patterns). Position in chain: between F4 (news-context) and F5 (risk-guard), as a final price-confirmation check before risk gates.

\paragraph{F9: Regime Viability Gate (H\^o Don Lee, 2014).}
Implements a \textit{regime-viability} notion that combines the practical trading principle of not forcing trades in structurally poor conditions \cite{miner2009highprobability} with the \textit{tradability} concept of \citeonline{lee2014tradability}: a setup may appear statistically admissible yet still not be operationally tradable if volatility is excessive, direction is absent, or the local state is unstable. The point of contact between the two references is the distinction between the existence of a signal and the existence of a market state in which executing that signal is justified. The present work adopts that conceptual distinction but not Lee's implementation details, which are too fragile for direct reuse: the original monograph relies on an Excel implementation, a 63-day sample, full Bloomberg dependence and a non-standard Tikhonov--Miller regularization step. The filter therefore re-specifies the idea in a cleaner and more scalable form through three sub-tests: (i) ATR within band ($\pm k$ standard deviations), (ii) ADF stationarity test on recent closes, (iii) sentiment polarity stability (fewer than $m$ sign flips in trailing window). Parameters: $k=2.0$, $\alpha_{\text{ADF}}=0.05$, $m=3$, lookback $n=60$ bars. Position in chain: first gate of Phase 2; if the regime is not viable, no further filters are evaluated. \textbf{Note:} F9 differs from F1 (trend-regime filter) in scope: F1 classifies trend direction (bull/bear/sideways), while F9 assesses whether the current regime is \textit{operationally tradable} regardless of direction. A market can be in a clear uptrend (F1 = bull) yet still untradable due to excessive volatility or instability (F9 = veto).

\paragraph{F10: Textual Confidence Gate (Ferrarini, 2025).}
Suppresses the news sub-model probability when the FinBERT scorer is uncertain. This gate is an engineering extension inspired by \citeonline{ferrarini2025aspectos}, who calibrates textual classifiers with ROC analysis and Youden's $J$ rather than accepting raw scores uncritically. Here that same calibration discipline is translated into a simple confidence rule based on the softmax margin (difference between top-2 class probabilities). If margin $< \theta_{\text{conf}}$, the news probability is neutralized to $0.5$ (no directional signal). Parameters: $\theta_{\text{conf}} \in [0.15, 0.30]$. Position in chain: Phase 1, between FinBERT scorer and news sub-model.

\paragraph{F11: Cross-Model Agreement Gate (Ferrarini, 2025).}
Reduces the weight of the news sub-model when FinBERT and the Loughran--McDonald dictionary scorer disagree strongly. This is likewise an adaptation inspired by \citeonline{ferrarini2025aspectos}: Ferrarini compares multiple textual models and explicitly suggests combined pipelines as future work, whereas the present dissertation translates that idea into a conservative agreement gate for financial news. If both models exhibit high confidence ($|s| > \theta_{\text{mag}}$) and opposite signs, the news weight multiplier is reduced to $0.5$ (or $0.0$ in a stricter variant). Parameters: $\theta_{\text{mag}} \in [0.30, 0.50]$. Position in chain: Phase 1, after F10.

\paragraph{F12: Sub-Model Consensus Gate (Veiga, 2022).}
Requires that at least $m$ of the 4 sub-models agree on direction. The gate is not claimed as a method proposed by \citeonline{veiga2022pso}; it is a conservative extension motivated by Veiga's explicit warning that optimization on stochastic trading processes is highly prone to overfitting. In the present work, that warning is operationalized by demanding minimum directional agreement across independently trained sub-models before execution is allowed. Each sub-model produces a calibrated probability; the gate counts BUY votes ($p > 0.55$), SELL votes ($p < 0.45$), and NEUTRAL otherwise. If $\max(\text{BUY}, \text{SELL}) < m$, the trade is vetoed. Parameters: $m \in \{2, 3, 4\}$, neutral band $[0.45, 0.55]$. Position in chain: Phase 2, after meta-learner, before trend-regime filter.

\paragraph{F13: Duplicate Detection Gate (Pugina, 2024).}
Discards news articles that are near-duplicates of recently processed items (syndicated wire stories). \citeonline{pugina2024jira} does not propose duplicate filtering; the contribution borrowed from that monograph is narrower and explicit: SBERT embeddings can be used as a practical text representation in a downstream decision pipeline. The present dissertation reuses that representation for a different purpose, namely cosine-similarity deduplication over the trailing window of recent headlines. If $\max(\text{sim}) > \theta_{\text{dup}}$ against the trailing $w$ embeddings, the article is skipped. Parameters: $\theta_{\text{dup}} = 0.95$, $w = 100$. Position in chain: Phase 1, before any sentiment scorer.

\paragraph{F14: Entity Relevance Gate (Brito, 2025).}
Filters news that fails to mention entities relevant to the currency pair (ECB, Fed, EUR, USD for EUR/USD; BOJ, JPY for USD/JPY). \citeonline{brito2025grafos} does not define pair-specific FX filters; its relevant contribution is the broader idea of using a semantic mediation layer to decide what information should reach later stages of processing. The present work adopts a lightweight version of that intuition through a simple keyword/entity gate with a predefined entity set and configurable minimum match count. Parameters: $\text{min\_matches} = 2$. Position in chain: Phase 1, early in the ingestion pipeline.

\paragraph{F17: Cross-Market Risk-Off Gate (La\"\i di, 2009; Chan, 2013).}
Blocks long USD/JPY (carry-direction) entries when cross-asset state signals risk-off conditions. The gate is motivated by the carry-trade unwind mechanism documented in \citeonline{laidi2009intermarket} (Ch.~5, pp.~115--126): structural current-account asymmetry makes JPY a funding currency, so volatility-of-volatility shocks force unwinding flows that move USD/JPY sharply against the carry direction. \citeonline{chan2013algo} (p.~184) provides a concrete VIX threshold: mean-reversion performance on the FSTX index dropped from 13\,\% APR to 2.6\,\% APR once $\text{VIX} > 35$. The filter combines three sub-checks in disjunction: (i) $\text{VIX}_t > 35$, (ii) rolling 20-day gold--USD correlation outside $[-1, -0.4]$ (anomalous regime relative to the structural $\rho \approx -0.84$ reported by \citeauthor{laidi2009intermarket}, p.~7), or (iii) entry timestamp inside the 21:00--01:00 UTC yen-unwind window (\citeauthor{laidi2009intermarket}, p.~123). Position in chain: Phase~2, after F9.

\paragraph{F18: S\&P Alignment Gate (La\"\i di, 2009; USD/JPY only).}
\citeonline{laidi2009intermarket} (Fig.~5.5, p.~120) documents a structurally positive correlation between USD/JPY and the S\&P~500 that emerged when the Fed began raising rates in June~2004 and persists through the present TCC window. The filter blocks entries whose proposed USD/JPY direction is contradicted by the previous day's S\&P~500 return by more than $1.5\sigma$. The gate is \emph{pair-specific}: it does not apply to EUR/USD, where the equity correlation is structurally weaker and not direction-stable. Position in chain: Phase~2, USD/JPY-only branch of the chain.

\paragraph{F19: Positioning Extreme Veto (Saettele, 2008).}
Adds a hard-data sentiment channel that is orthogonal to the FinBERT-derived NLP signal. \citeonline{saettele2008sentiment} (Ch.~5, pp.~83--91) constructs three positioning indices from the CFTC Commitments-of-Traders report (a Composite COT Index, a speculative \%Long Ratio Index and a commercial \%Long Ratio Index, all rank-transformed by percentile over a 52-week window) and reports that simultaneous alignment of the three indices at the 0 or 100 extremes coincided with major USD-index turns in 1989, 1992, 2001 and 2004. The present filter operationalizes that triple-alignment rule: if all three indices are $\geq 95$ (or $\leq 5$) and point against the proposed entry, the trade is vetoed. The convention of \citeauthor{saettele2008sentiment} (p.~76) is respected for USD/JPY: extreme bullish COT on JPY futures (CME) corresponds to a bearish signal on the USD/JPY spot. The filter adds a textual-independent sentiment channel; the present work does not adopt \citeauthor{saettele2008sentiment}'s keyword-based headline taxonomy (Ch.~4), which has been superseded by contextual scoring with FinBERT-class models. Position in chain: Phase~2, after F12.

\paragraph{F20: Data-Mining Significance Gate (Aronson, 2007).}
A configuration-time gate, not a runtime gate. The original design proposed a Sharpe-based permutation gate before promotion from validation to held-out testing. That gate remains unimplemented: the paired mean-return procedure in Section~\ref{subsec:rule-significance} is not a substitute for a selection-adjusted test of Sharpe superiority. The gate is configuration-level rather than execution-level because it acts on the full backtest history of a candidate variant rather than on per-bar state. The proposal follows the protective wrapper that \citeonline{aronson2007evidence} (Ch.~6, pp.~327--329) prescribes for any rule universe whose members share components and are therefore correlated, precisely the case of the F1--F19 chain. Historical design parameters: $M = 1{,}000$ permutations, $\alpha = 0.05$; these are not validated promotion criteria. Position: offline, between validation-span training and test-span evaluation.

\noindent\textbf{Effort prioritization.}
F10, F11 and F14 are \textit{zero-dependency} additions (~45 lines, no new packages) and can be implemented in a single session. F8 and F12 require TA-Lib functions already present. F9 requires \texttt{statsmodels} for the ADF test. F13 requires \texttt{scikit-learn} for cosine similarity and \texttt{sentence-transformers} for SBERT encoding. The recommended implementation order is: F10 $\rightarrow$ F11 $\rightarrow$ F14 $\rightarrow$ F8 $\rightarrow$ F12 $\rightarrow$ F9 $\rightarrow$ F13.

\subsection{Strategy variants for ablation} \label{subsec:strategy-variants}

Six strategy configurations are planned for the ablation stage of Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core:} F1--F7 only (baseline).
    \item \textbf{Variant A:} Core + F8. Tests the marginal contribution of candlestick confirmation.
    \item \textbf{Variant B:} Core + F10 + F11. Tests quality gating on the textual perception layer.
    \item \textbf{Variant C:} F9 + F1--F7 + F12. Tests regime viability and sub-model consensus.
    \item \textbf{Variant D:} All quality gates (F9, F10, F11, F13, F14 in Phase 1; F1--F8, F12 in Phase 2). Tests full-stack quality gating (risk: overfiltering).
    \item \textbf{Variant E:} Variant D plus the cross-asset and positioning gates (F17, F18 on USD/JPY only, F19). Tests the marginal contribution of cross-market and hard-data sentiment channels that are orthogonal to the FinBERT-derived signal.
\end{itemize}

The proposed variants require the same declared validation protocol and execution assumptions before their differences can be attributed to the filter set. Neither the CPCV evaluation nor the proposed F20 gate is demonstrated by the current pilot. The number of variants alone does not establish an acceptable false-discovery risk; the search history and inferential method require the review in Section~\ref{subsec:rule-significance}.

This separation between the non-deterministic perception layer and the deterministic filter chain is the central methodological commitment of the work. The perception layer is allowed to be non-deterministic (a fine-tuned BERT model produces sentiment scores, and a larger language model may in future replace it for richer feature extraction) because its outputs are computed once and read deterministically at backtest time, through the read-through cache and the materialized execution store of Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture}, never recomputed inside the replay loop. The filter chain is rule-based and designed for reproducible replay. Point-in-time validity still depends on the upstream data-availability audit. Given the same cached features and the same calibrated thresholds, the entire backtest is reproducible regardless of any non-determinism in the upstream perception layer. The pattern is therefore one of \textit{agentic perception, deterministic execution}: the perception layer can incorporate increasingly capable models without the trade-decision chain losing its auditability, its reproducibility; statistical validity still requires the evaluation gates. The migration path toward more capable perception layers is recorded in the conclusion as future work.

\section{Backtesting and Evaluation Protocol} \label{sec:evaluation}

The target evaluation protocol follows the methodological discipline laid out in \cite{lopezdeprado2018advances}. The following commitments specify the intended full study. The fixed-model pilot implements chronological separation and boundary purging, but not the rolling-refit or CPCV schedule.

First, walk-forward training. The data are split chronologically: 2015 to 2022 forms the training span, 2023 the validation span, and 2024 the held-out test span (subject to the coverage rule of Section~\ref{subsec:coverage-rule} possibly tightening the lower bound). Within the training span, models are refit on a rolling basis: at each refit point, the training window ends, a new validation slice is held out, and the model is retrained on data strictly preceding the slice. No future information is allowed to enter the feature values or the model parameters at any timestamp. Within each chronological split, the roles of the spans are fixed: the LightGBM family sub-models of Section~\ref{subsec:late-fusion} are fitted on the training span only; the logistic meta-learner is fitted on the validation span, on the sub-models' out-of-sample probabilities, so that the in-sample fit of the sub-models does not leak into the combiner; and the test span is never used for fitting. Because the label of Section~\ref{subsec:late-fusion} is computed over a forward horizon, every row whose label only becomes knowable after the end of its own span is purged, so that no fitted label depends on prices from a later, held-out span, and changing the held-out data cannot change what the models were fitted on.

Second, combinatorial purged cross-validation. Within each training window, validation folds are constructed using the combinatorial purged $k$-fold scheme of \citeonline{lopezdeprado2018advances}: feature observations whose label horizon overlaps any validation fold are purged from the training set, and an additional embargo period after each validation fold is excluded. This addresses the leakage path that arises when the dependent variable is computed over a forward horizon.

Third, transparent reporting. Every reported result identifies the data span, the validation scheme and the metrics. The metrics are: the annualized Sharpe ratio (after subtracting an explicit transaction-cost model), the maximum drawdown, the hit rate (proportion of trades closed in profit), the average holding time, and the turnover. The Sharpe ratio alone is too easily inflated by selection bias on short horizons \cite{lopezdeprado2018advances}; the supplementary metrics constrain the interpretation of any reported headline number.

The target transaction-cost model is empirical. Its calibration and its correspondence to the engine settings must be verified before a pilot is described as an economically validated simulation. From the tick Parquet, the rolling distribution of bid--ask spreads is computed for each pair. Each simulated trade incurs a half-spread cost on entry and on exit, plus a fixed slippage term calibrated against the larger end of the realized-spread distribution. Commissions are absorbed into the spread for the demo-account regime under which the back-tested data are obtained. The same cost model applies to the baseline and to the hybrid strategy, so that the comparison reports the marginal contribution of textual features net of identical execution friction.

\subsection{Adaptive Recency-Weighted Retraining} \label{subsec:adaptive-retraining}

The walk-forward protocol above fixes the family/combiner/test roles of a single chronological split. A separate, additionally registered study asks a narrower question within that same protocol: given the rolling-refit commitment already stated, does weighting recent rows more heavily than older ones, or truncating the training history altogether, change the terminal filter's (F7, Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution}) out-of-sample behaviour relative to an unweighted expanding window? This subsection documents the registered methodology for that comparison as planned; Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} reports results when available. No profitability claim, no numerical result and no outcome of any run is asserted here.

\paragraph{Lifecycle.} The adaptive protocol separates a host-side coordinator, which owns data ingestion, label-maturity tracking, retraining and publication, from the execution engine, which owns the trading account and order state. Concretely: newly downloaded price partitions are consumed and validated against a monotonically advancing availability watermark; labels mature once their forward horizon (Section~\ref{subsec:late-fusion}) has fully elapsed; at each registered refresh boundary the family sub-models and the meta-learner are fit and validated against the support minima stated below; a successful fit is published as an immutable bundle (model weights, resolved thresholds and full provenance) through an atomic write, never overwritten in place; and the running strategy loads the published bundle on demand for its next decision. The account and its open protective orders remain continuous across a bundle swap: a retrain never closes, resets or otherwise disturbs a position already open, and the swap only changes which model and thresholds decide future bars.

\paragraph{Five policies.} Holding the strategy configuration, filters, costs, sizing and exits fixed, five policies are registered that differ only in which model and thresholds the terminal filter uses at each month:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{F (frozen):} the family and combiner are fitted once, at the study's first epoch, and never refit; the thresholds are frozen at their initial calibration.
    \item \textbf{Q (frozen model, refreshed thresholds):} the same frozen family/combiner as F, but $\theta_{\text{high}}$ and $\theta_{\text{low}}$ are recalibrated every month.
    \item \textbf{R (rolling window):} the family and combiner are refit every month on a uniform trailing 180-day window, with thresholds refreshed monthly.
    \item \textbf{U (uniform expanding):} the family and combiner are refit every month on a uniform expanding window from the start of the training data, with thresholds refreshed monthly.
    \item \textbf{E (exponentially weighted expanding):} the same row support as U, but each row's contribution to the family and combiner fit is weighted by recency (below), with thresholds refreshed monthly and unweighted.
\end{itemize}

E versus U isolates the marginal effect of recency weighting on identical row support; R versus U isolates the effect of truncating history; Q versus F isolates the effect of threshold refresh alone, holding the model fixed.

\paragraph{Temporal contract.} Let $D$ denote a UTC calendar-month start (the deployment date) and $C = D - 1\,\text{day}$ (a one-day preparation embargo separating the last usable data point from deployment). For every policy that refits, the family-fitting span ends at $C - 60\,\text{days}$; policy R's family span additionally starts $180$ days before that end date ($C - 60\,\text{days} - 180\,\text{days}$), while policies U and E expand from a fixed start of \texttt{2015-03-02T00:00:00Z}. The combiner is calibrated on the half-open span $[C - 60\,\text{days},\, C - 30\,\text{days})$, using the family sub-models' out-of-sample predictions on that span, and the thresholds $\theta_{\text{high}}, \theta_{\text{low}}$ are calibrated on the following half-open span $[C - 30\,\text{days},\, C)$. The resulting bundle is deployed over $[D,\, D')$, where $D'$ is the following month's deployment date. All spans are half-open and expressed in UTC; a row joins a span only if its feature-availability timestamp falls inside the span and its label matures before the span's end, so no span admits a row whose outcome could only be known after the span closes. The registered study covers twelve monthly deployment epochs from \texttt{2016-03-01T00:00:00Z} up to, but excluding, \texttt{2017-03-01T00:00:00Z}, that is, March 2016 through February 2017: one continuous trading year per policy, with no gap or overlap between consecutive epochs.

\paragraph{Recency weighting.} For policy E, each training row $i$ receives a raw weight $w_i = 2^{-\mathrm{age\_days}_i / h}$, where $\mathrm{age\_days}_i$ is the elapsed calendar time, in fractional days, between the row's feature-availability timestamp and the fitting span's end, and $h = 60$ elapsed days is the half-life: a row sixty days older than the most recent admitted row contributes half the weight of that most recent row, a row one hundred twenty days older a quarter, and so on. The half-life is fixed a priori and is not tuned against any evaluation outcome. Weights are then normalized to mean one within each fitting stage (family and combiner) independently, $w_i' = n \, w_i / \sum_j w_j$, so that each stage's weights sum to its own row count and the regularization scale of the underlying LightGBM and logistic estimators is left unchanged by the reweighting. The corresponding effective sample size, $n_{\mathrm{eff}} = \left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2 \big/ \sum_i w_i^2$, is reported alongside every epoch as a weight-concentration diagnostic: it summarizes how unevenly the exponential schedule distributes influence across rows, and it is \emph{not} a count of independent observations, since consecutive minute-level rows within a fitting span remain highly autocorrelated regardless of how they are weighted. Threshold calibration is unweighted for every policy, including E: $\theta_{\text{low}}$ and $\theta_{\text{high}}$ are the strict-inequality 0.10 and 0.90 linear sample quantiles of the meta-learner's out-of-sample probabilities on the threshold span, so that the recency schedule affects only which rows the family and combiner models are fitted on, not how the terminal decision rule of Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution} is calibrated.

\paragraph{Support minima and activation.} A candidate epoch is fitted only if its family span has at least 1{,}000 rows with at least 50 rows per label class and $n_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 200$; the combiner span requires at least 100 rows with at least 20 per class and $n_{\mathrm{eff}} \geq 50$; the threshold span requires at least 100 rows. Any violated minimum rejects the whole epoch candidate for that policy, and the run reports the measured and required counts rather than silently relaxing the requirement. Consistently with the fail-fast discipline of Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}, there is no last-good-model fallback: if an epoch's candidate bundle cannot be validated, the epoch is recorded as a failed candidate and the strategy does not silently continue trading a stale or substitute model. Newly published bundles activate strictly in the future: a bundle is available for decisions only from its deployment date onward, never retroactively applied to bars that were already decided under the previous bundle, so a retrain cannot alter a decision the running strategy has already made.

\paragraph{Fixed controls.} The five policies share one strategy configuration, one continuous account per policy across the full study year, and identical filters, transaction costs, position sizing and exit rules (Sections~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution} and~\ref{sec:execution-engine}); only the F7 model and its two thresholds differ across policies and across epochs within a policy. This mirrors the controlled-comparison design of Table~\ref{tab:variables}: the manipulated variable is the retraining policy, and every other input to the terminal decision rule is held fixed so that a measured difference is attributable to the retraining schedule rather than to a confound in costs, sizing or the filter chain.

\paragraph{Analysis plan.} The primary registered endpoint is the mean paired UTC-daily net return difference between policies E and U over the common trading days of the study year, tested with the stationary block bootstrap of \citeonline{politis1994stationary}: a primary block length of four days, a sensitivity block length of two days, 9{,}999 resamples, seed 42, and a two-sided significance level of $\alpha = 0.05$. R minus U and Q minus F are reported as secondary, descriptive paired contrasts on the same daily grid, without a confirmatory hypothesis test. Independently of trading returns, prediction quality between E and U is reported on identical mature evaluation-row keys through log-loss, Brier score and directional accuracy, with the corresponding sample counts; consistently with Section~\ref{subsec:rule-significance}, classification-quality metrics are kept separate from, and are not treated as evidence of, trading profitability.

\paragraph{Exploratory status.} The study year (March 2016 through February 2017) has already been inspected in an earlier, unrelated pass of this dissertation's work, so this comparison cannot be presented as an untouched holdout in the sense of Section~\ref{subsec:rule-significance}: every finding it produces is exploratory, not confirmatory, and is reported and labeled as such regardless of the outcome. As with the rest of this chapter, everything above is a registered methodology, not a demonstrated result: the five-policy comparison is planned, and Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} reports the corresponding results once the registered runs and their evidence archive are available.

\section{Execution Engine} \label{sec:execution-engine}

The backtests of the present work target the open-source LEAN engine maintained by QuantConnect\footnote{\url{https://github.com/QuantConnect/Lean}}. The portion of the integration runnable at the time of submission is reported in Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}. Three properties drive the choice. First, LEAN is published under the Apache~2.0 license, which imposes no copyleft obligations on derivative work, a property that an eventual commercial deployment of the strategy can rely on. Second, an official OANDA v20 brokerage adapter\footnote{\url{https://github.com/QuantConnect/Lean.Brokerages.Oanda}}, also Apache~2.0, is available; the same engine therefore drives both the backtest reported in this work and any future paper-trading experiment. Third, LEAN exposes a Python algorithm interface, so strategy code can be expressed in the same language as the rest of the data pipeline. Should a hot path become Python-bound in future work, a Rust crate compiled via PyO3 can be loaded as a Python extension module and called from the strategy without changes to LEAN itself.

A clarification on cost is warranted, since the LEAN name is often associated with paid usage. That association concerns the QuantConnect \textit{cloud} platform (its hosted backtest and live-trading compute nodes, and its subscription Data Library) and not the engine. The present work uses neither: it runs the open-source LEAN engine \textit{locally}, through the LEAN command-line interface inside a Docker container, and feeds it data the project downloads and prepares itself (Section~\ref{sec:price-pipeline}). No QuantConnect cloud subscription, compute node or hosted dataset is involved, so backtesting, including the parameter and ablation sweeps that dominate the experimental work, incurs no platform cost beyond local compute. This is also why the data architecture of Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture} is built around a local, bulk-downloaded store rather than a hosted data feed. An eventual live deployment could either remain self-hosted, against the OANDA adapter, or adopt a paid cloud node; that decision lies outside the scope of the present work.

The alternatives considered and rejected illustrate the trade-offs. Backtrader is a mature Python backtester but is licensed under GPL v3, the viral copyleft of which is incompatible with future commercial reuse. NautilusTrader offers a modern Python/Rust architecture but lacks an OANDA brokerage adapter and is licensed under LGPL v3; both factors raise integration cost. Vectorbt is permissively licensed but provides only a research-style backtester without a live-execution layer. LEAN is the option that satisfies all of: permissive license, official Forex brokerage support, Python algorithm language, and runs natively on Linux.

The data are made available to LEAN through the durable \path{lean-data/} execution store discussed in Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture}. A Parquet-to-LEAN materializer performs the conversion: it writes a minute-resolution copy of the price Parquet, in the file layout LEAN's native data path expects, once and then reuses it across every run of a sweep; an optional \texttt{tmpfs} copy at \path{/dev/shm/lean-data/} may stage it in main memory for faster reads. The materializer's minute write path is implemented and pinned to a fixed engine image (\texttt{quantconnect/lean:17748}); an automated integration test runs that exact image in a container and confirms that bars written by the materializer are read back by the engine at their original instants. That test settled a convention the project now relies on, and which is not obvious from the documentation: LEAN stores Forex (OANDA) minute data in Coordinated Universal Time, indexed by each bar's start, so the canonical UTC Parquet maps one-to-one onto the engine's bars with no timezone offset. The materializer is a read-through, one-time tool per ingestion update: it runs on the first cache miss, takes a few seconds, produces on the order of one hundred megabytes per pair at minute resolution, and is not re-run per backtest. Sentiment and event features reach the strategy as the precomputed per-minute feature Parquet of Section~\ref{sec:feature-layer}, mounted into the engine container and read directly by the news-context filter; consistently with the read-through model of Section~\ref{sec:data-architecture}, they are computed once rather than on each run, so no model inference and no repeated transformation occur on the replay path.

Because the meta-learner is trained offline while the running algorithm reads LEAN's own indicators, parity between training and serving is enforced explicitly rather than assumed. Training features are computed over exactly the bar stream the engine delivers: LEAN's Forex market-hours calendar (for example, the daily 16:58--17:03 New York break, weekends and holidays, during which no bar is delivered) and its fill-forward of missing minutes from the previous close. Both paths compute the price features through the same feature-definition function, and the offline indicators are seeded as LEAN seeds them (the exponential moving average starts as a running simple average, and the MACD signal line starts once the slow average is ready). News features are keyed at each bar's decision time, the end of the bar, which is when the running algorithm observes it. An automated integration test replays fixture data inside the pinned engine container and verifies that, for every bar on which the running algorithm decides, its price and news features equal those of the corresponding training row to nine decimal places.

The capital-management and order-execution logic that sits on top of the deterministic decision layer (Section~\ref{subsec:deterministic-execution}) is planned as a clean-room implementation in Python: the code is to be written from scratch for this work, drawing on standard retail Forex algorithmic-trading practice, and without reference to any prior third-party codebase. The rules (Average-True-Range-based position sizing, partial-take-profit ladders, trailing-stop logic and exposure caps per pair) are standard constructions in retail Forex algorithmic trading and are to be implemented as Python modules compatible with the LEAN algorithm interface. The implementation status of each rule at the time of submission is reported in Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}.

\section{Reproducibility and Threats to Validity} \label{sec:reproducibility}

The present section organizes threats to validity along the four-bias taxonomy of \citeonline{hallsmoore2015}: optimization bias, look-ahead bias, survivorship bias and cognitive bias. To these are added two cross-cutting risks specific to the present pipeline, data coverage drift and process-related scope creep, discussed at the end of the section.

The principal data-related risk is coverage drift: a corpus that is densely populated in 2018 may thin out by 2024 or vice versa, distorting the time-series properties of the resulting sentiment series. The coverage rule of Section~\ref{subsec:coverage-rule} addresses this by deriving the training window from observed data rather than asserting it ex ante.

A secondary data-related risk is look-ahead leakage from headline timestamps. The GDELT and CC-News timestamps reflect publication time but the underlying article may report events from earlier, and aggressive timestamp harmonization can therefore introduce subtle leakage. More importantly, the publication timestamp alone is not the moment at which the corresponding textual feature is operationally available to a trading model: collection, scoring (the FinBERT inference, the Loughran--McDonald pass, the GDELT event-coding pipeline), aggregation into bucket-level features and propagation to the decision layer all add latency, none of which is captured by the bare publication timestamp. A backtest that consumes a feature at its publication minute therefore reads it earlier than any realistic live deployment could, biasing measured performance upward. The procedure adopted is conservative: every textual feature carries a fixed \textbf{publication-to-use delay of ten minutes}, that is, a news item published at minute $t$ becomes available to the model only from minute $t+10$ onward. The same rule applies to GDELT event aggregates: the daily aggregate that closes at the end of day $d$ is available to the model only from the start of day $d+1$ (Section~\ref{subsec:event-features}). A residual leak remains on that path and is recorded rather than hidden: the daily aggregate is keyed by the date on which each event occurred rather than the date on which GDELT added the record, so an event reported more than one day late is folded into a day whose aggregate is already visible. Closing it requires aggregating by the GDELT publication date instead, which the current events export does not yet carry. The ten-minute setting is deliberately conservative: it is large enough to absorb realistic delays in network fetching, inference, feature aggregation and read-through caching, and small enough that the intraday signal of news arrival is not erased. The conservative bias is preferred to a more aggressive setting because it favours reproducibility of the backtest in a live deployment over optimistic in-sample performance.

The principal model-related risk is backtest overfitting in the sense of \citeonline{lopezdeprado2018advances} and \citeonline{aronson2007evidence}: large hyperparameter searches over the same data tend to produce in-sample Sharpe ratios that are not informative about out-of-sample performance. \citeonline{aronson2007evidence} demonstrated this empirically by testing 6,400 binary rules on the S\&P 500 and showing that data-mined rules exhibit upwardly biased historical performance. The protocol therefore restricts hyperparameter search to a small explicit grid per sub-model and reserves the 2024 test span for a single evaluation pass per strategy variant. No iteration on the test span is permitted after the first pass.

\subsection{Rule-level significance under multiple comparisons} \label{subsec:rule-significance}

Cross-validation alone does not remove selection bias when many candidate strategies are screened on the same history \cite{aronson2007evidence,bailey2017overfitting}. The complete search history, including unsuccessful variants and repeated looks at evaluation results, must accompany inferential claims. The number of published variants is not necessarily the number of trials performed.

The corrected analysis uses net portfolio returns derived from actual LEAN equity, including open-position valuation, at explicitly recorded daily UTC boundaries (the close of the engine's end-stamped midnight candlestick, cross-checked against the engine's own daily return series when exported). Pair, evaluation window, cost treatment, risk-free convention, sampling grid and source hashes accompany each result. All endpoints are required: the analyzer neither interpolates missing equity nor substitutes closed-trade notional returns. The engine's annualized Sharpe remains descriptive, separate from the nonannualized return moments used for inference.

For the paired comparison, the estimand is the mean daily hybrid-minus-baseline net return, with a two-sided null of zero. The stationary bootstrap of \citeonline{politis1994stationary} resamples identical circular time blocks for both strategies, with geometric block lengths of declared expectation. Null-centered differences produce a two-sided bootstrap p-value and a compatible symmetric confidence interval; the interval is not studentized, so its coverage is approximate when the bootstrap error distribution is skewed. Block length and sensitivity settings must be specified before evaluation; all sensitivity outcomes are reported. At least thirty daily observations and ten expected blocks are required, so an expected block length $L$ needs at least $10L$ daily returns. Two candidate rules were registered before evaluation for ninety- and one-hundred-eighty-day windows, $L=\max\{1,\lfloor n^{1/3}\rfloor\}$ and the largest admissible length $\lfloor n/10\rfloor$; in the archived simulation study both exceeded the prespecified size bound (null rejection between 9\% and 14\% at the nominal 5\%), the first because short blocks under-capture serial dependence and the second because ten expected blocks are too few. Consequently, at those window lengths this procedure is not size-calibrated, and a confirmatory paired claim requires a materially longer evaluation window (the twenty-day setting passed only at twelve hundred observations) or a second-order accurate studentized variant, recorded as future work. The pilot's thirty-day window supports diagnostics only. Degenerate differences yield an unavailable statistic. The procedure assumes an adequately stationary, weakly dependent process with finite moments. Daily aggregation does not establish independence. A mean-return test does not test Sharpe superiority and therefore cannot by itself establish H1's risk-adjusted claim.

The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) of \citeonline{bailey2014deflated}, Eq.~2, is implemented as
\[
  \Pr(\widehat{SR}>SR_0)=\Phi\!\left(
  \frac{(\widehat{SR}-SR_0)\sqrt{T-1}}
  {\sqrt{1-\widehat{\gamma}_3\widehat{SR}
  +(\widehat{\gamma}_4-1)\widehat{SR}^{\,2}/4}}\right),
\]
where $\widehat{SR}$ is nonannualized, $T$ counts the same daily return observations,
$\widehat{\gamma}_3$ is skewness, and $\widehat{\gamma}_4$ is Pearson kurtosis.
The selection threshold $SR_0$ uses across-trial Sharpe dispersion and a documented
effective independent-trial count. It does not substitute the standard error of
one strategy for the dispersion across trials. One explicitly registered trial
uses $SR_0=0$ and returns the probabilistic Sharpe ratio, including $0.5$ at
$\widehat{SR}=0$. Missing selection history or unusable variance produces an
unavailable result, not an assumed single-trial experiment. The classical
expression alone does not adjust for arbitrary serial dependence.

Story 11 archives independent numerical fixtures, a registered AR(1) simulation
study, and saved-run integration diagnostics. These checks validate specified
software behavior, not trading profitability. Corrected reports use schema
version 2 and the field \texttt{deflated\_sharpe\_probability}; historical
Sharpe-scale adjustments and pooled-trade p-values remain explicitly
legacy/exploratory. Raw runs and frozen models are preserved, and outputs lacking
adequate equity or selection inputs are listed rather than silently regenerated.

These corrections do not repair other threats automatically: event-publication leakage, unvalidated cost assumptions, model-selection feedback, and insufficient regime coverage require separate evidence. CPCV and embargo remain target procedures rather than completed safeguards. Until these gates are met, Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} reports execution status and exploratory diagnostics without claiming that H1 has been established or rejected.

The principal process-related risk is scope creep relative to the available time budget. The defense date for the present work is set by the University of S\~ao Paulo at S\~ao Carlos and is treated as the manuscript-freeze deadline. The build is therefore sequenced as a layered minimum viable product (MVP), in three tiers.

The inner tier covers the price ingestion, the deterministic filter chain and the LEAN backtest on EUR/USD. It is non-negotiable. The second tier covers the FinBERT scorer with GDELT and the GPR regime feature. It converts the MVP from a price-only baseline into the hybrid strategy whose evaluation is the subject of Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation}. The outer tier covers the additional corpora discussed in Section~\ref{sec:news-pipeline}: FNSPID, CC-News, the central-bank releases and the Reddit Pushshift archive. The outer tier improves the hybrid without changing its architecture.

The sequencing is graceful. If the manuscript freeze arrives before an outer-tier item has been integrated, the corresponding section of the experimental chapter reports the work as documented future work rather than as silent omission. Furthermore, the unit-of-work abstraction in Section~\ref{sec:news-pipeline} guarantees that adding a deferred adapter post-defense does not invalidate the present results. The advantage of this layered approach is that the contribution of the work is preserved at every point along the schedule. A disadvantage is that the outer-tier items, which carry the most novelty in source coverage, are also the items most likely to be deferred.

A residual reproducibility concern is the closed access of some text sources. CC-News and FNSPID are openly redistributable; the Reddit Pushshift dumps remain available as historical archives; the Trump Twitter Archive is publicly maintained. The central-bank corpora are scraped from public web archives at fixed URLs. These source choices define the intended text-data pipeline; reproducible ingestion and coverage must be demonstrated for each source actually used.

The implemented LightGBM and logistic trainers fix their random state to 42.
The target seed policy for any additional stochastic component is recorded
below; the PyTorch entry does not imply that FinBERT inference ran in the pilot:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{numpy.random.seed(42)}
  \item LightGBM: \texttt{random\_state=42} on every \texttt{Booster}
  \item scikit-learn: \texttt{random\_state=42} on every estimator that
  exposes the argument
  \item PyTorch (for FinBERT inference): \texttt{torch.manual\_seed(42)};
  \texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms(True)} where supported by
  the CUDA back-end
\end{itemize}
Fitted models are persisted in a portable, pickle-free format: each
LightGBM sub-model in LightGBM's own text model format and the logistic
meta-learner as its coefficients and intercept, in a single JSON document
that also embeds the training provenance (training window and split
boundaries, label horizon, SHA-256 hashes of every input data partition,
source-code revision and library versions). Loading such a model executes
no code, it survives the older library versions shipped inside the pinned
engine container, and every backtest can be traced to the exact data and
code its model was trained on.
Library versions are pinned in the workspace \texttt{uv.lock}; the exact pins
in force at the time of submission are reproduced in
Chapter~\ref{cap:experimental-evaluation} alongside the experimental
results. The hardware environment is documented in the same chapter (CPU
model, GPU presence and driver version), since FinBERT inference times
depend on it.

\section{Final Remarks} \label{sec:methodology-final-remarks}

The methodology described in this chapter is engineering-led: every architectural choice (Parquet as the canonical layer, a durable \path{lean-data/} execution store materialized once for replay, late fusion of feature families, walk-forward training with combinatorial purged cross-validation, LEAN as the execution engine) is motivated by a stated constraint, and each open question (the FinBERT versus larger-foundation-model trade-off, the inclusion of optional corpora, the eventual transition to a transformer-based fusion model) is recorded as future work rather than assumed away. The next chapter reports the experimental evaluation that this pipeline produces.
